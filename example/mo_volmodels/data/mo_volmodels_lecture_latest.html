1   Market data and the implied-vol surface

MO options are European, cash-settled on the CSI 1000 index — the clean setting the three models assume (no early exercise). But live quotes do not come with a risk-free rate or a dividend yield attached: we must recover the carry from the prices themselves.

1.1   Put-call parity: recovering r, the forward, and the carry

For a given expiry, no-arbitrage forces a model-free linear relation across strikes:

C(K) − P(K) = DF · (F − K)   // straight line in K: slope = −DF, intercept = DF·F

A single least-squares fit of C−P against K yields the discount factor DF (hence r = −ln(DF)/T) and the forward F (hence the carry q = r − ln(F/S₀)/T). Here is what the MO chain actually implies:

Reading the market. The forward sits well below spot and falls with maturity — a steep negative basis. The recovered carry q is large (often 10–20%), not because the index pays such dividends, but because CSI 1000 futures trade at a deep discount, driven largely by structured-product (snowball) hedging flows. Note also that r is weakly identified at short maturities: since DF≈1, r=−ln(DF)/T amplifies quote noise by 1/T. The forward (the x-intercept) is robust; the rate (the slope) is not.

1.2   OTM selection and call-equivalent inversion

Only out-of-the-money options carry clean volatility information — deep-ITM quotes are dominated by intrinsic value and go stale. We keep OTM puts below the forward and OTM calls above it, then convert each OTM put to its call-equivalent price via parity, C = P + DF·(F−K), so a single Black inverter handles both wings and the two smiles agree at the forward by construction (the no-arbitrage property Dupire needs). 2   Dupire Local Volatility

Dupire (1994) asks: is there a deterministic instantaneous volatility σLV(S,t) such that a one-factor diffusion dS = (r−q)S dt + σLV(S,t) S dW reproduces the entire observed surface of European prices? The answer is unique and given by the forward equation (in implied total variance w = σ²T, log-moneyness y = ln(K/F)):

σLV²(K,T) = ∂Tw ÷ [ 1 − (y/w)∂yw + ¼(−¼ − 1/w + y²/w²)(∂yw)² + ½∂yyw ]

The denominator is a butterfly no-arbitrage term; the numerator is a calendar no-arbitrage term. Both must be positive. On real quotes they often are not:

Butterfly arbitrage in raw data. Building Dupire directly from the raw MO surface can raise a no-arbitrage rejection at wing and short-dated nodes — the second strike-derivative ∂yyw estimated on a coarse, bid/ask-quantized grid can produce a negative denominator. The builder refuses to fabricate a local vol there. The pipeline now does the desk-style repair upstream: fit a SABR smile to each expiry, evaluate a smooth rectangular IV grid, then project total variance to be non-decreasing in maturity before running Dupire with validation on. The model-calibration target is a SABR-smoothed, calendar-projected IV grid (beta=1.0); it differs from the raw rectangular grid by 0.96 vol-points RMSE and adjusted 0 total-variance nodes.

Repricing every OTM option through the local-vol PDE and re-inverting gives an overall IV RMSE of 4.52 vol-points against that smoothed target. In the continuous, arbitrage-free limit Dupire reprices the target surface exactly; the remaining gap is finite-grid PDE error plus the explicit choice to smooth noisy raw quotes before differentiating them.

3   Heston Stochastic Volatility

Heston (1993) makes variance itself stochastic — a mean-reverting CIR process correlated with spot:

dS = (r−q)S dt + √v · S dW₁
dv = κ(θ − v) dt + σ√v dW₂,    d⟨W₁,W₂⟩ = ρ dt

Five parameters shape the whole surface: v₀ and θ set the level (spot and long-run variance), κ the term structure of mean reversion, σ the vol-of-vol (smile convexity), and ρ the spot/vol correlation (skew). European prices follow from the characteristic function by Fourier inversion (Lewis form), which is what the fast calibrator uses.

Overall smile fit: 1.98 vol-points across all 6 expiries against the same smoothed target — and unlike raw Dupire this is achieved by an arbitrage-free stochastic model that cannot manufacture butterfly violations.

Two calibration lessons. (1) Identification. With only a handful of maturities the smile pins the combination σ²/κ, not κ alone — an unconstrained fit sends κ to any ceiling for marginal gain. We regularize (cap κ) rather than overfit. (2) Feller & numerics. The unconstrained fit is deeply Feller-violated (2κθ ≪ σ², so variance can hit zero), and that same extreme σ makes the downstream ADI PDE mis-price by ~2 vol-points. Adding a Feller penalty keeps 2κθ/σ² ≈ 1, costing a little smile fit but making both the calibration and the PDE trustworthy — the calibration objective and the numerical scheme are not independent choices.

4   Heston-SLV: the leverage function

Local vol reprices the smile but has unrealistic (deterministic) dynamics; Heston has realistic dynamics but cannot fit every smile exactly. SLV unifies them by multiplying the Heston vol by a deterministic leverage L(S,t):

dS = (r−q)S dt + L(S,t)·√v · S dW₁   // L chosen so E[v | S=K]·L(K,t)² = σ_LV²(K,t)

The calibration condition (Gyöngy) says the SLV model matches the market local variance iff L(K,t)² = σLV²(K,t) / E[vt | St=K]. We solve it by the forward Fokker-Planck method: evolve the joint (S,v) density one step at a time and read off the conditional expectation. The resulting leverage ranges L ∈ [0.52, 1.71] (L=1 would be pure Heston):

Which model for which product? The SLV PDE reprices these European vanillas to 4.65 vol-points — not better than analytic Heston. That is expected, not a failure: the SLV PDE carries a finite-grid discretization bias, while Heston vanillas use a semi-analytical Fourier pricer. SLV's value is not vanilla repricing — Heston already handles vanillas, exactly and cheaply. SLV earns its keep on exotics (barriers, autocallables, forward-starting and cliquet structures) where both the market smile and realistic forward-vol dynamics matter. The leverage surface above is the reusable deliverable.

5   Exotics: where the models diverge

Barrier option: forward-vol dynamics beyond the terminal smile

Every model so far was tuned to the same vanilla smile, and on vanillas they largely agree. A barrier option breaks that tie: its payoff depends on the path, hence on the forward-volatility dynamics — how volatility evolves after the spot moves — which the terminal smile alone does not pin down. We price a reverse up-and-out call (strike 8489, barrier 9338 ≈ 110% spot, weekly discrete monitoring — 24 weekly observations, T = 0.45) under all four models, each by Monte Carlo and PDE using the new quantark barrier engines. The MC column is the reference for the model-divergence story. The PDE column is an independent diagnostic: MC and PDE run on the same 260-step time grid, so both knock the barrier out on the identical weekly dates; the MC leg carries its standard error and the cross-check passes only when |MC − PDE| falls inside max(2% of price, 3 s.e.) — agreement is measured, not asserted.

This is why the model you calibrate is only half the decision: for a path-dependent exotic you must also choose the dynamics. This is the concrete reason SLV exists: it keeps the market smile and a realistic, tunable forward-vol process for exactly these products.

6   Delta-neutral hedging: same smile, different hedge behavior

Stage 09 takes BSM flat vol plus the calibrated LV, Heston, and SLV models and asks a desk question: if we are long one ATM European call (strike 8489, T = 0.70) and rebalance to delta neutral on the same deterministic down-up spot path, how do the hedge ratios differ? At each rebalance the stock/futures hedge is -model_delta. The residual PnL is computed as dV - previous_delta * dS; funding, transaction costs, and model recalibration are deliberately excluded so the chart isolates model-delta behavior. 7   Model comparison and takeaways

What is IV RMSE? For every quoted strike/maturity node, we first price the vanilla option under a candidate model, then convert that model price back into a Black implied volatility. The implied-volatility root mean square error is

IV RMSE = √ meanK,T ( σmodel-implied(K,T) − σmarket target(K,T) )²

We report it in vol-points: 1.00 vol-point means 1 percentage point of annualized implied volatility. This is useful because it compares all models in the same market language, independent of option notional, moneyness, and maturity price scale. A low IV RMSE means the model reproduces the terminal vanilla smile well; a high IV RMSE means the model is visibly missing the traded smile.

What IV RMSE can and cannot tell us. It is the right first diagnostic for separating flat BSM, Heston, Local Vol, and SLV on vanilla smile fit. It does not uniquely identify the forward-volatility dynamics. Two models can have similar IV RMSE and still produce different barrier prices, Snowball values, delta hedges, turnover, and residual PnL. That is why the lecture uses IV RMSE for the vanilla calibration story, then uses exotics and hedging to differentiate the dynamics.

The arc of the study. A flat-vol Black-Scholes leaves a 2.7 vol-point smile on the table. Local vol can in principle remove all of it, but only after the raw quotes are turned into a smooth no-arbitrage target. Heston, arbitrage-free by construction and Feller-regularized for numerical health, fits that target robustly to a few vol-points. SLV then layers a leverage surface on top so the model is simultaneously smile-consistent and stochastic — the right tool once you leave vanillas for exotics. The recurring theme: market data is not arbitrage-free, parameters are not fully identified, and the calibration objective must respect the numerics that will consume its output.
